## 19.1 — Translational Mechanical Systems

### 19.1.1 — The Effort-Flow Pair

From Ch. 17 §17.2.2:

- **Effort:** Force $F$ [N]
- **Flow:** Velocity $v$ [m/s]
- **Power:** $P = Fv$ [W]
- **Generalized momentum:** $p = \int F\,dt$ [N·s] = linear momentum
- **Generalized displacement:** $x = \int v\,dt$ [m]

**The three passive elements:**

| Element | Symbol | Constitutive law | Energy | Physical meaning |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mass $m$ | $L_{mech} = m$ | $F = m\dot v = m\ddot x$ | $\frac{1}{2}mv^2$ | Kinetic energy storage (inertia) |
| Spring $k$ | $C_{mech} = 1/k$ | $F = kx$, $v = \dot x = \frac{1}{k}\dot F$ | $\frac{1}{2}kx^2$ | Potential energy storage |
| Damper $b$ | $R_{mech} = b$ | $F = bv$ | $P_{diss} = bv^2$ | Viscous dissipation |

### 19.1.2 — The Single-DOF Mass-Spring-Damper

Newton's second law for a mass $m$ on a spring $k$ with damping $b$,
driven by force $F_{ext}(t)$:

$$\boxed{m\ddot x + b\dot x + kx = F_{ext}(t)}$$

This is exactly the universal second-order ODE from Ch. 17 §17.7.1, with:
$L = m$, $R = b$, $C = 1/k$, $q = x$, $e_{source} = F_{ext}$.

**Natural frequency and damping:**

$$\omega_0 = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}, \qquad \zeta = \frac{b}{2\sqrt{km}} = \frac{b}{2m\omega_0}, \qquad Q = \frac{\sqrt{km}}{b} = \frac{m\omega_0}{b}$$

**Common engineering systems and their $\omega_0$:**

| System | $\omega_0$ (rad/s) | $f_0$ (Hz) |
|---|---|---|
| Automotive suspension | 6–12 | 1–2 |
| Building floor (occupied) | 13–20 | 2–3 |
| Typical machine tool | 60–600 | 10–100 |
| MEMS accelerometer proof mass | $10^4$–$10^5$ | $10^3$–$10^4$ |
| Quartz crystal resonator | $10^7$–$10^8$ | $10^6$–$10^7$ |
| Atomic force microscope cantilever | $10^4$–$10^6$ | $10^3$–$10^5$ |

### 19.1.3 — Vibration Isolation

**Problem:** A rotating machine on a rigid floor vibrates at frequency $\omega$.
Isolate the floor from the vibration force.

**The transmissibility** $T(\omega)$ is the ratio of force transmitted to the
floor to the force generated by the machine:

$$T(\omega) = \left|\frac{F_{transmitted}}{F_{machine}}\right| = \sqrt{\frac{1+(2\zeta r)^2}{(1-r^2)^2+(2\zeta r)^2}}$$

where $r = \omega/\omega_0$.

**Key result:** For $r > \sqrt{2}$, $T < 1$ (isolation achieved). For $r < \sqrt{2}$, $T > 1$ (amplification).

**Design rule:** Mount the machine on springs such that $\omega_0 < \omega_{machine}/\sqrt{2}$.
For a fan at 3000 rpm ($\omega = 314$ rad/s): need $\omega_0 < 222$ rad/s → $f_0 < 35$ Hz.
For $f_0 = 5$ Hz → $r = 314/31.4 = 10$ → $T \approx 0.01$ (99% isolation).

**Damping trade-off:** High $\zeta$ reduces resonance peak during startup
(when machine passes through $\omega_0$) but worsens isolation at operating
frequency. Optimal: $\zeta \approx 0.05$–$0.15$ for continuous rotating machinery.

### 19.1.4 — Base Excitation

When the base (floor/ground) moves with displacement $y(t) = Y\cos\omega t$,
the absolute displacement of the mass $x$ and relative displacement $z = x - y$:

$$m\ddot z + b\dot z + kz = -m\ddot y = m\omega^2 Y\cos\omega t$$

**Transmissibility** (displacement ratio $|X/Y|$): same formula as force transmissibility.

**Engineering:** Seismic isolation of buildings and precision instruments.
A building on base isolators with $f_0 = 0.3$ Hz ($\omega_0 = 1.9$ rad/s):
at earthquake frequency $f = 1$–$10$ Hz ($r = 3$–$30$): $T \approx 0.001$–$0.1$.

## 19.2 — Rotational Mechanical Systems

### 19.2.1 — The Rotational Effort-Flow Pair

- **Effort:** Torque $\tau$ [N·m]
- **Flow:** Angular velocity $\omega$ [rad/s]
- **Power:** $P = \tau\omega$ [W]

**Elements:**

| Element | Law | Energy | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Moment of inertia $I_r$ | $\tau = I_r\dot\omega$ | $\frac{1}{2}I_r\omega^2$ | Flywheel, rotor, disk |
| Torsional spring $k_r$ | $\tau = k_r\theta$ | $\frac{1}{2}k_r\theta^2$ | Shaft, coupling |
| Rotational damper $b_r$ | $\tau = b_r\omega$ | $P = b_r\omega^2$ | Bearing friction, fluid damping |

Governing equation for a single-DOF torsional system:

$$I_r\ddot\theta + b_r\dot\theta + k_r\theta = \tau_{ext}(t)$$

Same second-order template; $\omega_0 = \sqrt{k_r/I_r}$, $\zeta = b_r/2\sqrt{k_rI_r}$.

### 19.2.2 — Gear Trains as Transformers

A gear pair with $N_1$ and $N_2$ teeth is a **mechanical transformer** (TF in bond graph):

$$\frac{\tau_2}{\tau_1} = \frac{N_2}{N_1} = n, \qquad \frac{\omega_1}{\omega_2} = \frac{N_2}{N_1} = n$$

Power conserved (ideal gear): $\tau_1\omega_1 = \tau_2\omega_2$.

**Reflected inertia:** A load inertia $I_{load}$ on the output shaft appears
at the input shaft as $I_{load}/n^2$ — the gear ratio squared. This is the
mechanical analog of the transformer's impedance transformation:

$$Z_1 = n^2 Z_2 \quad\text{(electrical)} \qquad I_{1,eff} = I_{load}/n^2 \quad\text{(mechanical)}$$

**Gear efficiency:** Real gears have friction losses. Efficiency varies from
$\eta_{spur} \approx 0.97$–$0.99$ per stage (helical gears); worm gears
can be as low as $\eta \approx 0.4$–$0.7$ (high reduction ratio but lossy).

### 19.2.3 — Shaft Critical Speed

A rotating shaft with an out-of-balance mass $m_u$ at eccentricity $e_u$
from the geometric center behaves as a forced harmonic oscillator.
At the **critical speed** $\omega_c = \sqrt{k_{shaft}/m_{rotor}}$, the shaft
deflects resonantly — potentially to failure.

For a simply-supported shaft with a central disk of mass $m$ on a shaft
of stiffness $k = 48EI/L^3$ (from beam theory):

$$\omega_c = \sqrt{\frac{48EI}{mL^3}}$$

**Design rules:**
- Operating speed well above or below $\omega_c$ (separation factor $> 1.3$)
- Supercritical design (above $\omega_c$): cross through critical speed quickly during startup; add damping to limit amplitude at $\omega_c$
- Used in: turbines, centrifuges, grinding spindles

**Flywheel energy storage:** $E = \frac{1}{2}I_r\omega^2 = \frac{1}{2}m r_{gyr}^2\omega^2$.
For a steel disk ($r = 0.5$ m, $m = 100$ kg) at $\omega = 3000$ rpm:
$I_r = \frac{1}{2}(100)(0.5)^2 = 12.5$ kg·m²; $E = \frac{1}{2}(12.5)(314)^2 = 616$ kJ.

## 19.3 — Multi-DOF Systems and Modal Analysis

### 19.3.1 — The Equations of Motion

For $N$ masses with displacements $\mathbf{q} = (q_1,\ldots,q_N)^T$,
the equations of motion in matrix form (from Ch. 9 §9.8 normal modes):

$$\mathbf{M}\ddot{\mathbf{q}} + \mathbf{C}\dot{\mathbf{q}} + \mathbf{K}\mathbf{q} = \mathbf{F}(t)$$

where $\mathbf{M}$, $\mathbf{C}$, $\mathbf{K}$ are the mass, damping, and stiffness matrices.

**Free undamped vibration** ($\mathbf{C} = 0$, $\mathbf{F} = 0$):

$$\mathbf{M}\ddot{\mathbf{q}} + \mathbf{K}\mathbf{q} = \mathbf{0}$$

Try $\mathbf{q} = \mathbf{v}\,e^{i\omega t}$ → generalized eigenvalue problem:

$$(\mathbf{K} - \omega^2\mathbf{M})\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}$$

The $N$ eigenvalues $\omega_n^2$ are the natural frequencies squared; the $N$
eigenvectors $\mathbf{v}^{(n)}$ are the **mode shapes**.

### 19.3.2 — Modal Decomposition

Transform to modal coordinates $\mathbf{q} = \boldsymbol{\Phi}\boldsymbol{\eta}$
where $\boldsymbol{\Phi} = [\mathbf{v}^{(1)}\vert\cdots\vert\mathbf{v}^{(N)}]$:

$$\ddot\eta_n + 2\zeta_n\omega_n\dot\eta_n + \omega_n^2\eta_n = f_n(t)/m_n^*$$

**Each mode is an independent single-DOF oscillator** — with its own $\omega_n$,
$\zeta_n$, and modal mass $m_n^* = \mathbf{v}^{(n)T}\mathbf{M}\mathbf{v}^{(n)}$.

This is the engineering application of the eigenfunction expansion from Ch. 16 §16.5:
the normal mode decomposition is the same operation whether applied to a lumped
mass-spring system (this chapter) or a continuous elastic body (Ch. 15–16).

### 19.3.3 — Vibration Absorber (Frahm Dynamic Absorber)

Add a small mass-spring subsystem ($m_a$, $k_a$) tuned to the excitation
frequency $\omega_{exc}$: set $\omega_a = \sqrt{k_a/m_a} = \omega_{exc}$.

At $\omega = \omega_a$: the absorber mass oscillates 180° out of phase,
creating a force that exactly cancels the excitation on the main mass.
The main mass becomes perfectly stationary.

**Design:** $k_a = m_a\omega_{exc}^2$. Typical mass ratio $\mu = m_a/m_{main} = 0.05$–$0.20$.

**Applications:** Building sway dampers (Taipei 101: 660-tonne pendulum
absorber at $f_0 = 0.15$ Hz); bridges (Millennium Bridge London: TMDs after
pedestrian resonance event); machining chatter suppression.

## 19.4 — Power Transmission and Machine Elements

### 19.4.1 — Power Flow in Mechanical Transmissions

Any power transmission chain: prime mover (engine/motor) → transmission
(gears, belt, chain) → load (pump, generator, wheel).

**Power balance:** $P_{output} = \eta_{total}\,P_{input}$

**Efficiency chain:** For $n$ elements: $\eta_{total} = \eta_1\times\eta_2\times\cdots\times\eta_n$

Typical efficiencies:
- Helical gear stage: 98–99%
- V-belt: 93–97%
- Chain drive: 97–99%
- Worm gear: 40–95% (depends on lead angle)
- Ball screw (rotation→translation): 90–95%

A drivetrain with five elements at 97% each: $\eta_{total} = 0.97^5 = 0.86$ — 14% loss.

### 19.4.2 — The Ball Screw as a Transformer

A ball screw converts rotation (effort = torque $\tau$, flow = $\omega$)
to translation (effort = force $F$, flow = velocity $v$):

$$v = \frac{p}{2\pi}\omega, \qquad F = \frac{2\pi\tau\eta}{p}$$

where $p$ is the lead (m/revolution) and $\eta$ is the efficiency.

This is a **mechanical transformer** with ratio $n = p/2\pi$ [m/rad]:
$e_2 = e_1/n$ (force from torque), $f_2 = n\cdot f_1$ (velocity from angular velocity).

**Application:** CNC machine axes, industrial robots, hydraulic press actuators.
Typical ball screw: lead = 5–20 mm/rev, efficiency 90–95%, dynamic load rating
determines life (L10 life from Hertz contact stress at ball-raceway interface).

## 19.5 — Thermodynamic Cycles in Engineering

### 19.5.1 — The Framework: Carnot and Real Cycles

From Ch. 10 §10.6: any heat engine operating between $T_H$ and $T_C$:

$$\eta_{Carnot} = 1 - \frac{T_C}{T_H}$$

All real cycles are less efficient. The gap measures irreversibility from:
friction, heat transfer across finite temperature differences, incomplete combustion,
non-quasi-static processes.

**The thermodynamic cycle in effort-flow language:** The working fluid carries
energy. Effort = pressure $P$ (or temperature $T$ for heat exchange), flow =
volume flow rate (or entropy flow $\dot S = \dot Q/T$). The cycle traces a
closed path in the $P$-$v$ (pressure-volume) or $T$-$s$ (temperature-entropy) diagram.

### 19.5.2 — Rankine Cycle (Steam Power)

The workhorse of electrical power generation (coal, nuclear, concentrated solar):

| Process | Device | Change |
|---|---|---|
| 1→2 | Pump | Isentropic compression of liquid |
| 2→3 | Boiler | Constant-pressure heat addition (evaporation + superheat) |
| 3→4 | Turbine | Isentropic expansion (work output) |
| 4→1 | Condenser | Constant-pressure heat rejection (condensation) |

**Thermal efficiency:**
$$\eta_{Rankine} = \frac{W_{turbine} - W_{pump}}{Q_{boiler}} = 1 - \frac{Q_{condenser}}{Q_{boiler}}$$

**Improvements:**
- **Superheat:** Raise $T_3$ → higher $\eta$, drier steam in turbine
- **Reheat:** Expand partway, reheat, expand again → higher $T_{avg}$ in cycle
- **Regeneration:** Extract steam from turbine to preheat feedwater → less heat needed from boiler

Modern supercritical coal plant: $T_H = 600°$C, $T_C = 40°$C,
$\eta_{Carnot} = 64\%$, actual $\eta \approx 40$–$47\%$.

### 19.5.3 — Brayton Cycle (Gas Turbine, Jet Engine)

| Process | Device | |
|---|---|---|
| 1→2 | Compressor | Isentropic compression |
| 2→3 | Combustor | Constant pressure heat addition |
| 3→4 | Turbine | Isentropic expansion |
| 4→1 | Exhaust | Constant pressure heat rejection |

**Efficiency as a function of pressure ratio $r_p = P_2/P_1$:**

$$\boxed{\eta_{Brayton} = 1 - \frac{1}{r_p^{(\gamma-1)/\gamma}}}$$

For $r_p = 30$, $\gamma = 1.4$: $\eta = 53\%$ (ideal). Actual: 35–45%.

**Why gas turbines can achieve high $T_H$:** Turbine inlet temperature (TIT)
now exceeds 1700°C in modern aero-engines — well above the melting point of
the blade alloy. Possible through: thermal barrier coatings, internal cooling
channels with compressed air, single-crystal Ni superalloy blades (no grain
boundaries → no creep at high temperature). This is materials physics (Ch. 6
bonding + diffusion, Ch. 13 Arrhenius) in direct engineering service.

### 19.5.4 — Otto and Diesel Cycles (Internal Combustion)

**Otto cycle** (gasoline, spark ignition):

$$\boxed{\eta_{Otto} = 1 - \frac{1}{r^{\gamma-1}}}$$

where $r = V_{max}/V_{min}$ is the compression ratio.
For $r = 10$, $\gamma = 1.4$: $\eta_{Otto} = 60\%$ (ideal). Actual: 25–35%.

**Diesel cycle** (compression ignition, constant-pressure combustion):

$$\eta_{Diesel} = 1 - \frac{1}{r^{\gamma-1}}\cdot\frac{r_c^\gamma - 1}{\gamma(r_c-1)}$$

where $r_c = V_3/V_2$ is the cutoff ratio. For $r_c < $ corresponding Otto ratio:
$\eta_{Diesel} < \eta_{Otto}$ at same compression ratio — but diesel engines
run at higher $r$ (16–22 vs. 9–12 for gasoline) → typically higher actual $\eta$.

### 19.5.5 — Vapor Compression Refrigeration Cycle

Inverse of the Rankine cycle: work input pumps heat from cold to hot.

**COP (Coefficient of Performance):**
$$COP_{refrigerator} = \frac{Q_C}{W_{compressor}} = \frac{T_C}{T_H - T_C}$$
(Carnot limit)

For a room air conditioner ($T_C = 20°$C, $T_H = 35°$C):
$COP_{Carnot} = 293/15 = 19.5$. Actual: 3–6.

**Heat pump COP:**
$$COP_{heat\,pump} = \frac{Q_H}{W_{compressor}} = COP_{refrigerator} + 1$$

A heat pump with $COP = 4$ delivers 4 kW of heat for 1 kW of electrical input —
4× more efficient than a direct electric heater. Governed by the same Carnot
limit as everything else.

**Refrigerants:** The thermodynamic cycle efficiency depends on the refrigerant's
$T$-$s$ diagram shape (specific heat, latent heat, saturation curve). HFCs (R-134a,
R-410A): high GWP → replacing with low-GWP alternatives (R-32, R-1234yf, CO₂).

## 19.6 — Heat Transfer in Engineering Practice

### 19.6.1 — The Three Modes and Their Resistances

From Ch. 13 §13.5 and Ch. 14 §14.7, all three heat transfer modes have
thermal resistance representations:

**Conduction:**
$$\dot Q = \frac{\kappa A}{L}\Delta T = \frac{\Delta T}{R_{cond}}, \qquad R_{cond} = \frac{L}{\kappa A}$$

**Convection:**
$$\dot Q = hA(T_{wall} - T_{fluid}) = \frac{\Delta T}{R_{conv}}, \qquad R_{conv} = \frac{1}{hA}$$

The convection coefficient $h$ is not a material property — it depends on
flow conditions (Re, Pr) and must be found from correlations.

**Radiation:**
$$\dot Q = \sigma\epsilon A(T_1^4 - T_2^4) \approx h_{rad}A(T_1 - T_2)$$

where $h_{rad} \approx 4\sigma\epsilon T_m^3$ linearized around the mean temperature $T_m$.
For $T_m = 350$ K, $\epsilon = 0.9$: $h_{rad} \approx 7.5$ W/m²K —
comparable to natural convection.

### 19.6.2 — Dimensionless Numbers for Convection

**Reynolds number** $Re = \rho vL/\mu$ (from Ch. 15 §15.8.1):
determines flow regime (laminar/turbulent) in the convecting fluid.

**Prandtl number** $Pr = \mu c_p/\kappa = \nu/\alpha$:
ratio of momentum diffusivity to thermal diffusivity.

- $Pr \ll 1$ (liquid metals): thermal diffusion much faster than momentum → wide thermal BL
- $Pr \approx 1$ (gases): thermal and velocity BLs similar thickness
- $Pr \gg 1$ (oils, viscous fluids): thermal BL much thinner than velocity BL

**Nusselt number** $Nu = hL/\kappa$: ratio of convective to conductive heat transfer.
All convection correlations express $Nu$ as a function of $Re$ and $Pr$:

**Dittus-Boelter (turbulent pipe flow, $Re > 10^4$, $0.6 < Pr < 160$):**

$$Nu_D = 0.023\,Re_D^{0.8}\,Pr^n, \qquad n = 0.4\;\text{(heating)},\; 0.3\;\text{(cooling)}$$

**Churchill-Chu (natural convection on vertical plate):**

$$Nu_L = \left[0.825 + \frac{0.387\,Ra_L^{1/6}}{[1+(0.492/Pr)^{9/16}]^{8/27}}\right]^2$$

where $Ra = Gr\cdot Pr = g\beta\Delta T L^3/\nu\alpha$ is the Rayleigh number.

**Biot number** (from Ch. 16 §16.10.1): $Bi = hL/\kappa_{solid}$

$Bi \ll 1$ (≤ 0.1): temperature uniform in solid → lumped capacitance valid
$Bi \gg 1$: significant temperature gradient → solve heat equation

### 19.6.3 — Heat Exchangers: LMTD and NTU Methods

A heat exchanger transfers heat from a hot fluid (inlet $T_{H,in}$, outlet $T_{H,out}$)
to a cold fluid (inlet $T_{C,in}$, outlet $T_{C,out}$).

**Overall heat transfer coefficient $U$:**

$$\frac{1}{UA} = \frac{1}{h_H A_H} + \frac{\ln(r_o/r_i)}{2\pi\kappa L} + R_{fouling} + \frac{1}{h_C A_C}$$

(thermal resistances in series: hot-side convection + wall conduction + fouling + cold-side convection)

**LMTD method** (when inlet and outlet temperatures are known):

$$\dot Q = UA\cdot\text{LMTD}, \qquad \text{LMTD} = \frac{(T_{H,in}-T_{C,out}) - (T_{H,out}-T_{C,in})}{\ln\frac{T_{H,in}-T_{C,out}}{T_{H,out}-T_{C,in}}}$$

**NTU-effectiveness method** (when outlet temperatures are unknown — design problem):

$$\varepsilon = \frac{\dot Q}{\dot Q_{max}} = \frac{\dot Q}{C_{min}(T_{H,in}-T_{C,in})}$$

$$NTU = \frac{UA}{C_{min}}, \qquad C_r = \frac{C_{min}}{C_{max}}$$

For a counterflow heat exchanger:
$$\varepsilon = \frac{1 - e^{-NTU(1-C_r)}}{1 - C_r e^{-NTU(1-C_r)}} \quad (C_r < 1)$$

**Engineering:** A shell-and-tube heat exchanger designed with $NTU = 2$,
$C_r = 0.5$ achieves $\varepsilon = 0.80$ (80% of maximum possible heat transfer).
The NTU-effectiveness framework connects directly to the thermal circuit (R-network)
of Ch. 14 — $NTU = UA/C_{min}$ is the ratio of thermal conductance to heat capacity flow rate.

## 19.7 — Fluid Systems as Hydraulic Circuits

### 19.7.1 — The Hydraulic Effort-Flow Pair

From Ch. 17 §17.2.2 and Ch. 15:

- **Effort:** Pressure $P$ [Pa] (or pressure difference $\Delta P$)
- **Flow:** Volume flow rate $Q$ [m³/s]
- **Power:** $P = \Delta P\cdot Q$ [W]

**Hydraulic R/C/L elements:**

| Element | Law | Physical |
|---|---|---|
| Pipe resistance $R_{hyd}$ | $\Delta P = R_{hyd}Q$ | Hagen-Poiseuille: $R = 8\mu L/\pi r^4$ |
| Accumulator $C_{hyd}$ | $Q = C_{hyd}\dot P$ | Fluid compressibility or elastic vessel: $C = V/\rho c_s^2$ |
| Fluid inertance $L_{hyd}$ | $\Delta P = L_{hyd}\dot Q$ | Accelerating/decelerating fluid column: $L = \rho L/A$ |

**KCL analog (mass conservation):** $\sum Q_k = 0$ at every junction.
**KVL analog (energy/pressure):** $\sum\Delta P_k = 0$ around every loop
(modified by elevation: $\Delta P + \rho g\Delta h = 0$).

### 19.7.2 — Pipe Network Analysis

Pipe networks (water distribution, HVAC, hydraulic machinery) are analyzed
exactly as electrical resistor networks:

1. Apply KCL (flow balance) at each junction node
2. Apply KVL (pressure balance) around each loop: $\sum_k \Delta P_k = 0$
   where $\Delta P_k = R_k Q_k$ for each pipe
3. **Nonlinearity:** For turbulent flow, $\Delta P = fL\rho Q^2/(2DA^2) \propto Q^2$
   (Darcy-Weisbach); the resistance is not constant → iterative solution (Hardy Cross method)

**Minor losses:** Fittings, valves, bends add equivalent length $L_{eq} = KD/f$:

| Fitting | $K$ |
|---|---|
| Gate valve (fully open) | 0.1 |
| Globe valve (fully open) | 6–10 |
| 90° elbow (standard) | 0.9 |
| 90° elbow (long radius) | 0.4 |
| T-junction (branch) | 1.5 |

### 19.7.3 — Pump and System Curves

A pump's **characteristic curve** $H(Q)$ (head vs. flow rate) and a pipe system's
**system curve** $H_{sys}(Q)$ (pressure drop vs. flow) intersect at the operating point.

**Pump curve:** $H_{pump} = H_0 - AQ^2$ (centrifugal pump, parabolic)

**System curve:** $H_{sys} = H_{static} + BQ^2$ (static head + friction losses)

**Operating point:** $H_{pump} = H_{sys}$ → solve for $Q^*$, $H^*$.

**Pump similarity laws (for geometrically similar pumps of different size or speed):**
$$\frac{Q_2}{Q_1} = \frac{N_2}{N_1}\left(\frac{D_2}{D_1}\right)^3, \qquad \frac{H_2}{H_1} = \left(\frac{N_2}{N_1}\right)^2\left(\frac{D_2}{D_1}\right)^2, \qquad \frac{P_2}{P_1} = \left(\frac{N_2}{N_1}\right)^3\left(\frac{D_2}{D_1}\right)^5$$

These follow from dimensional analysis (Ch. 15 §15.8) — the Reynolds similarity
and Froude similarity conditions applied to turbomachinery.

## 19.8 — Acoustics: Sound and Vibration

### 19.8.1 — Sound as a Wave

From Ch. 15–16: acoustic pressure $p'$ satisfies:

$$\frac{\partial^2 p'}{\partial t^2} = c_s^2\nabla^2 p'$$

with $c_s = \sqrt{\gamma P_0/\rho_0}$ (speed of sound in a gas).

**Sound pressure level (SPL):**
$$L_p = 20\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{p_{rms}}{p_{ref}}\right)\;\text{dB}, \qquad p_{ref} = 20\;\mu\text{Pa}$$

| Source | SPL (dB) |
|---|---|
| Threshold of hearing | 0 |
| Library | 30–40 |
| Conversation | 60 |
| Factory floor | 80–95 |
| Threshold of pain | 130 |
| Jet engine (1 m) | 150 |

**Acoustic impedance** $Z_{ac} = \rho c_s$: analogous to electromagnetic wave
impedance $Z_{EM} = \sqrt{\mu/\epsilon}$ (Ch. 11 §11.8). Reflection at interfaces
from impedance mismatch — the same Fresnel formula as optics (Ch. 12 §12.3.3).

### 19.8.2 — Room Acoustics: Reverberation

A room of volume $V$ and total sound absorption $A_{room}$ (Sabins):

$$RT_{60} = 0.161\frac{V}{A_{room}}$$

**Sabine formula** for reverberation time (time for sound to decay 60 dB):
$A_{room} = \sum S_i\alpha_i$ where $S_i$ is the area and $\alpha_i$ the absorption
coefficient of each surface.

**Absorption coefficients $\alpha$ at 500 Hz (typical):**
- Concrete: 0.02 (nearly perfect reflector)
- Carpeted floor: 0.30
- Upholstered seats: 0.60
- Acoustic foam: 0.80–0.99

**Optimal RT60:** Concert halls: 1.8–2.2 s; speech/lecture: 0.5–1.0 s;
recording studios: 0.2–0.5 s.

### 19.8.3 — Vibration Isolation vs. Noise Control

**Vibration isolator** (mechanical): see §19.1.3.
Targets: prevent machine vibration from radiating as sound, or prevent ground
vibration from reaching sensitive equipment.

**Active noise cancellation:** An additional sound source (loudspeaker)
generates the anti-phase signal to cancel the primary noise at the observer's ears.
Works for correlated, low-frequency noise (up to $\sim$ 500 Hz):
- Aircraft cabin: 8–12 dB reduction in engine noise
- In-ear headphones (ANC): 20–30 dB reduction of low-frequency ambient noise
- Requires real-time digital signal processing (DSP, from Ch. 18) with
  feed-forward or feedback control (Ch. 21 preview)

## 19.9 — Thermofluid Systems as Coupled Networks

### 19.9.1 — Multi-Domain Bond Graph: HVAC

An HVAC system couples hydraulic (refrigerant circuit), thermal (heat transfer),
and mechanical (compressor, fans) domains:

**Refrigerant circuit (hydraulic domain):**
- Compressor: effort source (pressure head) driving refrigerant flow
- Condenser: hydraulic resistance + thermal interface to air
- Expansion valve: controlled variable resistance (throttle)
- Evaporator: hydraulic resistance + thermal interface to air

**Thermal domain:**
- Condenser: TF (transformer) coupling hydraulic to thermal: $\dot Q = \dot m_{ref}\Delta h_{cond}$
- Evaporator: TF coupling hydraulic to thermal: $\dot Q_{cooling} = \dot m_{ref}\Delta h_{evap}$

**The coupling:** the refrigerant mass flow rate $\dot m$ connects the hydraulic
circuit power ($\dot m\Delta P$) to the thermal circuit power ($\dot m\Delta h$)
through enthalpy change $\Delta h$ at each heat exchanger.

The bond graph for the complete HVAC system has exactly the same mathematical
structure as a multi-domain electrical-mechanical coupled system — the same
junction rules, the same power conservation, the same transfer function analysis.
